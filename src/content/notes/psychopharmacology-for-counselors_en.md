> This essay grew out of the workshop "One-Day Intensive: Psychopharmacology", held in September 2025 for graduate assistants at the Mental Health Guidance Center of Fujian Medical University. It is written for researchers, counsellors, and students. It does not provide diagnosis, prescribing, or individualized medication advice.

The workshop did not begin with receptor diagrams or a classification table. It began with overdose and an ordinary cough medicine. That is closer to the way medication enters counselling. Someone may first notice that they cannot stay awake during the day, sit still, concentrate, or keep their usual sleep pattern. Sometimes the ingredients on the box receive close attention only after an episode of overuse.

Dextromethorphan makes this problem unusually visible. It is a centrally acting non-opioid antitussive with established medical uses, but it may also be taken for psychoactive effects or become involved in poisoning and self-harm. A [US emergency department surveillance study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10917081/) estimated that adverse events involving over-the-counter cough and cold medicines accounted for about 26,735 visits per year between 2017 and 2019, with 61.4% involving non-therapeutic use. Those figures cover cough and cold medicines as a category in the United States. They do not describe China or the incidence of dextromethorphan use alone. Their narrower lesson is still worth noting: familiar and readily available medicines can become part of misuse, self-harm, and exposure to several substances at once.

A molecule's name is not enough to tell us what it means clinically. In 2022, the US FDA approved [Auvelity, an extended-release combination of dextromethorphan and bupropion](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/215430s009s018lbl.pdf), for major depressive disorder in adults. The indication belongs to that specific combination product. It does not make an ordinary cough preparation suitable for self-treating depression. Formulation, dose, indication, other medicines, and medical supervision place the same molecule in a very different clinical context.

## How medication enters counselling

Counsellors do not diagnose or prescribe, and they should not advise someone to start, adjust, or stop a medicine on their own. [Article 23 of the Mental Health Law of the People's Republic of China](https://www.npc.gov.cn/c2/c30834/201905/t20190521_281477.html) defines relevant professional boundaries and requires counselling personnel to recommend medical assessment when a mental disorder may be present. Those boundaries establish responsibility. They do not remove the experience of taking medication from counselling.

Sedation, agitation, changes in appetite or weight, sexual dysfunction, withdrawal reactions, and movement abnormalities all reach into daily life. A student may be unable to stay awake through the morning. Akathisia may make a work meeting almost impossible to sit through. Sexual side effects or emotional blunting may alter a relationship. Psychopharmacological knowledge helps a counsellor ask questions that clarify timing. Did the change begin before or after the medicine was started? Did it follow a higher dose, missed doses, a switch, or discontinuation? Does "no motivation" include marked sedation? Does "worse anxiety" include an inability to sit still? Might reluctance to continue treatment relate to weight, sexual side effects, cost, or stigma?

These observations cannot establish a medical cause, but they can make the next medical appointment more informative. Dates of medication changes, changes in function that followed, and the person's own interpretation usually tell a clinician more than the general report that things have felt worse lately.

This was why the workshop did not keep every medicine inside a receptor-based sequence. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors such as fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, citalopram, and escitalopram, along with serotonin and norepinephrine agents such as venlafaxine and duloxetine, often enter counselling through accounts of sleep, gastrointestinal symptoms, fluctuating anxiety, or difficulty initiating tasks. Atomoxetine, methylphenidate, and bupropion bring attention, arousal, and task completion into the conversation. Mirtazapine, trazodone, and vortioxetine are often discussed in relation to sleep, appetite, and daytime functioning. Mechanisms help us understand these experiences, but the experiences do not arrive sorted like a pharmacology textbook.

The effects of antipsychotic treatment are often first noticed in the body. Clozapine, risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, paliperidone, and amisulpride are used in different clinical situations, yet the drug name alone says little about what a person is going through. Restlessness may be mistaken for anxiety. Slowed movement and sedation may be interpreted as a lack of effort. Changes in weight, menstruation, sexual function, or the felt speed of thought may affect shame, intimacy, and the therapeutic alliance. A [systematic review of reasons for antipsychotic discontinuation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28424542/) found that attitudes towards medication, side effects, cognitive difficulties, substance use, and therapeutic alliance can all influence discontinuation.

Lithium, valproate, and lamotrigine draw attention to long-term monitoring. Blood concentrations, renal and thyroid function, metabolic and reproductive safety, and a new rash or mucosal symptoms each have clinical significance. Monitoring also costs time and money. Blood tests, travel, registration, waiting, and fees can determine whether a pharmacologically reasonable regimen remains workable in everyday life.

Sedatives and anxiolytics carry a different kind of complexity. Buspirone and tandospirone are non-benzodiazepine anxiolytics. Lorazepam, clonazepam, and alprazolam are benzodiazepines. Zopiclone and zolpidem are usually classed as Z-drugs, while daridorexant and lemborexant act at orexin receptors. Gabapentin and pregabalin are used mainly for epilepsy or neuropathic pain, but they may also appear where anxiety, insomnia, pain, and nonmedical use overlap. Physical dependence is not the same as addiction. Tolerance, withdrawal, and the psychological role that a medicine gradually takes on still deserve careful attention. [NICE guidance on dependence and withdrawal](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng215/chapter/Recommendations) recommends collaborative, non-blaming communication and advises against abrupt cessation without medical guidance.

## From pharmacology back to daily life

Pharmacology can explain receptors, metabolism, and adverse effects. It cannot by itself explain why someone begins, continues, or stops taking a medicine. Drowsiness may undermine study or work; weight change may affect body image; sexual dysfunction may alter a relationship. Emotional blunting can even change how someone understands their identity and recovery. These are not separate "psychological factors" added after the biology. They begin in bodily experience and unfold within a person's circumstances.

Price, insurance, travel to follow-up, supply, family supervision, school rules, shift work, and online information all shape access and patterns of use. A missed dose may follow an unstable routine. Concealment may reflect stigma or fear of disciplinary consequences. Peers, online communities, and purchasing channels may influence nonmedical use. Biological, psychological, and social factors are therefore more useful as one unfolding process than as three lists. A bodily change disrupts daily life, and that disruption may shape the next dose.

Changes to dextromethorphan regulation in mainland China show how institutions enter that process. A [2021 announcement](https://m.cnpharm.com/c/2021-12-27/813307.shtml) changed oral single-ingredient dextromethorphan hydrobromide from over-the-counter to prescription status. The [2022 prohibited list for online medicine sales](https://yjj.sh.gov.cn/qtgzwj/20221201/2f5354ead1604574b9f9a3cf7099fb35.html) included these products. In 2024, a [national management notice](https://yjj.sh.gov.cn/qtgzwj/20240522/0d73f1eda509486b9b40bbde9f93cbe0.html) placed dextromethorphan salts and single-ingredient preparations under second-category psychotropic substance controls. A separate [2026 notice on yumei preparations](https://yjj.sh.gov.cn/qtgzwj/20260422/80e65f492ae049dc801b852beb18a4cd.html) added a ban on online sales, real-name registration, restrictions on sales to minors, traceability requirements, and closer monitoring. Here, yumei refers specifically to products containing only dextromethorphan and guaifenesin, so the notice should not be confused with the broader 2024 controls.

Regulation can restrict access, but it cannot remove the motives behind misuse, self-harm, or the search for psychoactive effects. When one route closes, use may move to another medicine or purchasing channel. [Clinical adverse event reports](https://ccj.pku.edu.cn/article/info?aid=364520522) and [in-depth interviews with adolescents](https://rkyfz.pku.edu.cn/Article/info?aid=373373906) in parts of China have documented nonmedical dextromethorphan use and related risks. Without a nationally representative denominator, these sources cannot estimate prevalence across China. They tell us which risk pathways appeared in the reported cases and interviews, no more than that.

## Recording information that remains useful

A fuller conversation can begin with what the person is actually taking. Prescription medicines, combination cold remedies, herbal preparations, supplements, alcohol, and other substances all belong in the record. Brand and generic names, timing, dose, source, and original packaging can help. "I only took some cough medicine" may still mean exposure to acetaminophen, an antihistamine, or another active ingredient in a combination product.

The medication history can then be placed on the same timeline as changes in daily life. When was a medicine started, increased, reduced, switched, missed, or stopped? When did sleep, appetite, sexual function, movement, gastrointestinal symptoms, attention, or emotional activation change? A counsellor does not have to decide whether the medicine caused the change. Recording the sequence and functional consequences clearly is already useful for medical follow-up.

It is also worth asking what the medicine was doing for the person at that moment. Cough suppression, sleep, self-soothing, maintaining study, seeking dissociation, and self-harm involve different risks and different needs for support. Neutral, non-stigmatising questions are more likely to elicit reliable information. Counselling can make room for mixed feelings about benefit, side effects, dependence, stigma, and long-term treatment. It can also help someone prepare questions for the next medical appointment. Decisions to start, adjust, or stop medication remain with medical professionals.

## When medical care is urgent

Difficulty waking, a progressive decline in consciousness, respiratory distress or marked slowing of breathing, seizures, syncope, severe agitation, or delirium require emergency care and a call to 120. High fever accompanied by rigidity, tremor, myoclonus, or hyperreflexia may indicate serotonin toxicity or another serious reaction. The [FDA label for Auvelity](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/215430s009s018lbl.pdf) warns that serotonin syndrome can occur when dextromethorphan is combined with certain serotonergic medicines. High fever, marked rigidity, altered consciousness, and autonomic instability during antipsychotic treatment also need urgent assessment.

A new widespread rash, blisters, or mucosal injury after lamotrigine; a marked worsening of tremor, ataxia, vomiting, or altered consciousness during lithium treatment; and any intentional overdose or medication-related self-harm should also lead promptly to medical evaluation. [General poison first aid guidance](https://www.poison.org/first-aid-for-poisonings) advises against inducing vomiting. Keep medicine packages, remaining tablets, and purchase information where possible, and tell emergency personnel about every substance taken. The task at the scene is to secure safety and referral, not to perform a detailed pharmacological interpretation.

## Questions that remain open

Psychopharmacological knowledge can prevent bodily or cognitive changes from being read as purely psychological. It can also prevent the opposite error, in which a handful of mechanisms is used to explain too much. A more reliable account places medication changes alongside relationships, identity, daily rhythms, and access to care, then follows how these factors affect one another over time.

That kind of observation leads naturally to research questions. Does weight change affect adherence through body image or shame? How do shift work, sleep, and medication timing shape treatment stability? Under what conditions do online communities spread information about nonmedical use, and when do they become routes to help-seeking? Research needs to distinguish therapeutic from nonmedical use, occasional from repeated use, accidental overdose from self-harm, and exposure to one substance from exposure to several.

[openFDA's explanation of adverse event reports](https://open.fda.gov/apis/drug/event/) offers a useful methodological caution. Spontaneous reporting systems can detect safety signals, but they cannot estimate incidence or establish causation by themselves. The same restraint helps in counselling. A counsellor can record what changed, when it changed, and how daily life was affected, then bring that information into the appropriate medical conversation without exceeding the evidence or the limits of the role.
